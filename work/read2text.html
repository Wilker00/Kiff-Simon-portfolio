Read2Text — homework a specialist can trust.

Speech-language pathologists (SLPs) and reading specialists still send worksheets home. They can’t tell if the practice happened. Read2Text is AI fluency practice — anytime, on any device — with progress the adult can actually see.

Research signal

Ten conversations reframed the product around two very different jobs.

Interviews with ten SLPs and reading specialists surfaced manual progress tracking as the recurring pain point. Student usability sessions then exposed where the practice flow felt confusing or too clinical.

Educator discovery

Educators described hours lost each week assigning paper packets and trying to reconstruct practice adherence after the fact.

Decoupling user roles

The specialist needed longitudinal analytics and assignment management. The student needed a focused, low-friction reading session.

Usability iteration

Early navigation confusion led to a simplified onboarding sequence, distinct role entry paths, and fewer barriers before practice.

Context

Worksheets don’t give you a graph.

Traditional fluency work is repeated reading on paper. Engagement drops. Progress is a guess. Read2Text exists so homework is verifiable and practice can happen outside the session.

The UX problem is split: a student who is anxious about reading aloud, and an adult who needs evidence without another admin chore.

Constraints

It has to work for a tired specialist and a kid on a phone.

If assignment takes longer than printing a packet, it loses. If the student surface feels like a test, they won’t open it. Accessibility wasn’t a badge — it was the product.

Decisions

Key architectural & product decisions

Consumer app vs. clinical homework

A cute reading companion is a different product. We stayed on assignable, verifiable practice so SLPs could replace the worksheet, not compete with bedtime apps.

One login vs. two jobs

The specialist needs data. The student needs a short path to reading. Mixing those in one dense screen would fail both. Separate jobs, shared assignment.

Wireframe with the people in the room

I didn’t invent fluency pedagogy. Educators and developers named the failure mode. The interface had to serve that, then look calm enough to use daily.

Reflection

What I’d tighten

I would still want a session sitting next to an SLP during after-school assignment — the moment the product either saves them time or becomes another tab. That’s the test the Figma file can’t fake.
